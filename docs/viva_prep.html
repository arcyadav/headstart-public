Viva preparation: HeadStart, a study planner for your semester

Everything needed to explain and defend Project 2: what HeadStart does and why, how planning a semester becomes an optimisation problem, how the CP-SAT engine solves it, the design decisions, what two weeks of real use showed, and the questions an examiner is likely to ask, with answers.

Live site: headstart-1g49.onrender.com · Code: github.com/arcyadav/headstart-public

1. The project in 2 minutes

Say this if asked "explain your project":

"HeadStart is a study planner built for one semester of a student's life. You give it your subjects and weekly timetable, your exams and assignments, and the times you're free to study. It then plans when to study each thing, in sessions of sensible length with breaks, so that everything is finished before its deadline, and it re-plans whenever you log a session, finish a task or a class is cancelled.

Under the hood, planning is an optimisation problem: thousands of 10-minute time slots, dozens of tasks, deadlines, dependencies and daily limits. I solve it with Google's CP-SAT constraint solver in two stages: first decide how many minutes of each task go on each day, then place those minutes into exact time slots for each day.

I used it myself through two weeks of midsems. My CMO midsem went from 16.5/40 (below average) to 32/45 (median 23). One student and one exam, so not proof, but my own logs show steady study before that exam. What really helped wasn't the exact order of sessions, it was the workload forecast: seeing how much each subject needs by when, which creates urgency early.

It's deployed as a demo site anyone can try, and for real use it runs on your own computer, including from your phone over Wi-Fi, so your data never leaves your devices."

2. Problem and goal

The problem

Students want to study regularly but often fail to. They

  • don't know how much time to give each task,
  • waste time making schedules instead of following them, or
  • give up when they miss a session, because the plan falls apart.

And a semester's work never stops arriving: a new lecture every few days, assignments and quizzes on top. A to-do list doesn't tell you when; a static timetable breaks the first time you miss something.

The goal

  • Take the timetable, free time, subjects, exams, tasks and hobbies.
  • Generate "study this lecture" work from the timetable by itself.
  • Plan so work finishes as early as the student's daily limits allow, never past a deadline.
  • Re-plan instantly when anything changes, without reshuffling everything.
  • Always answer: what should I do next, and why?

How it evolved: v0.1 (29 Sep) was a generic "smart balanced scheduler" for tasks. On 30 Sep I redesigned it for students (spec v0.2): work organised by subject, exams as dated milestones rather than tasks, lectures generated from the timetable, and "as soon as possible" instead of "evenly spread". Everything after that came from using it.

3. What it does

Planning

  • Week, month and (on a phone) one-day views of the plan.
  • "Next" card: the session to do now, with a reason ("CMO Midsem in 2 days").
  • Lecture items made automatically after each class (per subject, on/off).
  • Revision before each exam (default 6 h; quiz 1 h), in exam order.
  • Exam focus window: the 24 h before an exam is for that subject only.
  • Problems panel: what can't fit before its deadline, and by how much.

Day-to-day

  • Log a session (time worked, time left) or mark tasks done; undo for 15 s.
  • Cancel, move or add an extra class; edit exams, tasks, revision hours.
  • Fixed-time events (club, gym), hobbies with weekly targets.
  • Task types: study, assignment, other. Exams or quizzes.
  • Settings: paint free time on a weekly grid, daily max/min hours, session lengths.

Insight (from your own log)

  • Today tally, streaks, hours per day vs. your daily minimum.
  • Exam effort: hours that went into each exam, by type of work.
  • When you study: hours by hour of day and weekday; your best 3-hour stretch.

Where it runs

  • On your computer (desktop shortcut, no terminal).
  • On your phone over Wi-Fi: QR code in Settings, Add to Home screen.
  • Online demo on Render that saves nothing.

4. Demo script (about 3 minutes)

  1. Week view on the laptop. "These are my real classes (grey), exams (red) and planned sessions in each subject's colour. The shaded band is an exam focus window."
  2. Next card. "It always tells me what to do now and why."
  3. Log a session (click it → time worked, time left → Save). "It re-plans in a few seconds, and only what changed moves: the rest of my week stays put."
  4. Subjects tab. Show a subject's weekly classes, the lecture items they generate, an exam, a task with its type. "Exams are milestones: lectures before an exam count toward it automatically."
  5. Problems panel (if anything is listed). "It never squeezes work in silently: if 2 hours don't fit before my midsem, it says so, and I can raise a day's limit or cut work."
  6. Stats. Exam effort (e.g. CMO Midsem ≈ 15 h) and When you study (22:00–01:00).
  7. Phone. Open the home-screen icon: "same planner, over Wi-Fi from my laptop".
  8. Live site. Open the demo, set a semester, add a subject: "anyone can try it; it saves nothing."

Before the viva: open the live site once (the free server sleeps after 15 minutes and takes about a minute to wake). Start HeadStart on the laptop and check the phone link in Settings on the room's network (or use your phone hotspot).

6. The maths: modelling a semester

Time

Time is cut into 10-minute slots from now to the horizon (the latest exam or deadline, at least 7 days ahead). Free slots = study slots you painted − classes − exams − fixed events. Windows too short for any session (e.g. 15 min between classes) are dropped. A real 5-week plan has about 2,300 free slots.

Why 10 minutes: fine enough for real schedules (a 50-min session, a 10-min break), coarse enough that the model stays small. 1-minute slots would make it 10× bigger for no practical gain.

Work

Every piece of work is an item with a remaining need (in slots), an optional finish-by (its due date, or the exam it counts toward), an optional available-from (a lecture only after its class), and dependencies:

  • Hard: B can't start before A is done (always kept).
  • Soft: e.g. revision after the lectures, or this exam's revision after the previous exam's: kept unless that would break a deadline.

Decision variables (stage 1)

a[u,d] ∈ ℤ≥0 slots of item u planned on day d n[u,d] ∈ ℤ≥0 number of sessions of u on day d z[u,d] ∈ {0,1} u is used on day d h[k,d] ∈ ℤ≥0 slots of flexible weekly activity k (hobby) on day d unscheduled[u], short_work[d], short_week[k,w], broken[e] : slack variables

Constraints (all linear, or linear when a boolean is true)

RuleConstraint
All work is either planned or reportedΣ_d a[u,d] + unscheduled[u] = need[u]
Deadline / available-froma[u,d] only exists on days (and day-parts) where u may run
Session lengthmin_len·n[u,d] ≤ a[u,d] ≤ min(max_len, longest window)·n[u,d]
Day capacity (with breaks)Σ_u a[u,d] + 1·Σ n[u,d] + Σ_k h[k,d] ≤ free[d] + 1
Daily study limitΣ_u a[u,d] ≤ max_work[d]
Daily minimum (soft)Σ_u a[u,d] + short_work[d] ≥ min_work[d]
Sessions per windowΣ n ≤ Σ_windows ⌊(len + break) / (shortest + break)⌋
Dependency A→Bif z[B,d] (and not broken[e]): Σ_{d'≤d} a[A,d'] + unscheduled[A] ≥ need[A]
Weekly hobby targetΣ_{d∈week} h[k,d] + short_week[k,w] = target[k,w]

Objective: one weighted sum, priorities by weight

minimise 10000·Σ unscheduled (work that doesn't fit: worst) + 1000·Σ short_week (hobby targets missed) + 500·need·Σ broken (soft order broken) + 50·Σ work in the 1-day buffer before a deadline + 20·Σ short_work (under the daily minimum) + 5·Σ |a − average| (uneven spread, steady items only) + 1·Σ urgency·weight·(d − earliest day)·a[u,d] (finish early) + 1·Σ |a − previous plan| (stability)

The weights are spaced so a higher goal always wins: one slot of unscheduled work (10,000) costs more than any realistic amount of the goals below it. This is a practical stand-in for a lexicographic objective.

Two linear-programming tricks used

  • Absolute value (spread, stability): add dev ≥ x − c and dev ≥ c − x and minimise dev; at the optimum dev = |x − c|.
  • Conditional constraints: "only if z is true" (OnlyEnforceIf in CP-SAT), the solver's version of the big-M trick, used for dependencies and broken soft edges.
  • Slack variables: every goal that can fail has one, so the model is always feasible and the non-zero slacks are the problem report ("2 h of DAA work doesn't fit before 12 Oct").

7. CP-SAT and optimisation background

What kind of problem is this?

A scheduling problem with deadlines, precedence and capacity: an integer optimisation problem. In general such problems are NP-hard (they contain bin packing: fitting session-sized pieces into windows of free time), so there is no known fast exact algorithm for every case. In practice, real instances of this size solve in seconds with a good solver.

Why CP-SAT?

  • It handles integer and boolean variables natively, plus logical conditions ("if this task runs today, its prerequisite is done"). Plain LP can't; MILP can but needs big-M tricks.
  • It combines constraint propagation, a SAT solver with clause learning (it learns why a partial plan failed and never tries that combination again), and LP relaxations for bounds, running several strategies in parallel workers.
  • It gives an optimality gap: the best plan found and a proven bound on how good any plan can be. Stage 1 stops once within 1% of the bound.
  • It's free, well maintained (Google OR-Tools) and fast for this size.

Alternatives and why not

ApproachWhy not (alone)
Greedy (earliest deadline first)Fast but myopic: can't trade today vs. tomorrow, ignores session lengths and breaks globally, can't prove a plan is impossible. I do use a greedy layout as a fallback and as the solver's starting point.
Pure LPCan't express "a session is 45–120 min" or "if… then…" without integers.
MILP (e.g. CBC/Gurobi)Possible; CP-SAT is usually faster on scheduling with many booleans and logical constraints, and is free.
Machine learningNothing to learn from at the start, and a plan must guarantee hard rules (deadlines, limits); ML predicts, an optimiser guarantees.
One big model with every slotCorrect but huge: slot-level variables for every item over 5 weeks is far bigger and too slow to re-plan on every click. Hence the two stages.

8. The two-stage engine

Stage 1: allocate (the whole horizon at once)

Decides how many minutes of each item go on each day: deadlines, dependencies, daily limits, hobbies, finishing early, stability. ~2,200 variables, ~3,800 constraints for a real 5-week semester; optimal in about 1 s.

Stage 2: place (each day separately, in parallel)

Puts each day's minutes into exact 10-min slots: x[p,s] = 1 if piece p is in slot s. Sessions within min/max length, a break between different work, at most max-length of work before a break, same-day dependency order, fewer and earlier sessions preferred. Largest day: ~800 variables.

Stage 2 in maths

Σ_p x[p,s] ≤ 1 one thing per slot Σ_s x[p,s] + miss[p] = want[p] place it all (or report) start[p,s] = x[p,s] − x[p,s−1] ⇒ x[p,s..s+L−1] = 1 sessions ≥ L slots x[p,s] + x[q,s+1] ≤ 1 (p ≠ q, both work) a break between Σ work in any (max_len+1) consecutive slots ≤ max_len a break after max length minimise 100000·Σ miss + 100·Σ starts + Σ s·x + 20·Σ moved

Feedback between the stages

Stage 1 works with day totals, so occasionally a day's minutes don't fit into its actual windows (e.g. two sessions that would need a gap that isn't there). Then stage 1 is solved again with that item capped on that day at what did fit, and the rest moves to another day (up to 5 rounds; each round starts from the last answer). This is a simple form of decomposition with feedback cuts. Usually one round is enough.

Robustness (a real bug from using it)

On 7 Oct my whole day came out empty and planning took ~3 minutes. Cause: I'd set a 13 h limit on a day with only ~11 h free, so the day was packed to ~91%; stage 2 ran out of its 2 s search time with nothing found, and the code treated "no answer yet" as "nothing fits", capping the day at zero. Fix: a rule-respecting greedy layout is kept whenever the search times out (never an empty day), and each re-plan round is warm-started from the previous one. Re-plans went from ~193 s to ~35–50 s; today a full re-plan is ~5 s.

Stable re-plans

Without care, every re-plan could reshuffle the whole week, which is useless to a student. So the previous plan is a hint (the search starts from it) and moving work costs a little: 1 per slot moved between days in stage 1, 20 per slot moved within a day in stage 2. Result: re-planning with nothing changed moves nothing; a done task, new task or cancelled class changes only what it has to.

9. Design decisions (and why)

DecisionWhy
As soon as possible, not evenly spreadWork keeps arriving; free time today is needed tomorrow. Front-loading builds a lead that absorbs missed sessions. Each slot later costs more, weighted by urgency (1–5 by days to deadline).
Target finish 1 day before the deadlineA buffer for the unexpected; working inside it is allowed but penalised. Revision has no buffer: the evening before an exam is when revision happens.
Exams are milestones, not tasksAn exam is a date; the work for it is lectures, assignments and revision that point to it. Lectures before an exam count toward it automatically.
Exam focus window (24 h)The day before an exam is only for that subject; two close exams each keep their own stretch.
Never overloadThe daily limit is never exceeded. If work doesn't fit, it is reported, never silently squeezed in.
Two files + log, recompute the planSimple, debuggable, undo for free, data for Stats.
Online = demo onlyI didn't want to store other students' schedules. Per-user online planners were built (14 Oct) and deliberately removed. Real use runs locally; data stays on your device.
Phone via the laptop (Wi-Fi), not the cloudSame privacy reason; the phone is a second screen for the same planner.
Removed "Your pace"I built a feature that learned how long tasks really take, used it, found it wasn't useful in practice, and removed it. Data showed my estimates were already close (e.g. RP lectures: plan 1h30, take 1h32).

10. Real use and results

Used it daily for about two weeks of midsems (from 30 Sep), and through the semester after. Fixes came from real problems, not imagined ones: an empty day (solver timeout), a "Mark done" crash, an exam saved as 2016, Chrome refusing http on the phone.

Outcome

  • CMO midsem: 16.5/40 → 32/45 (median 23). Honest framing: one student, one subject, a different exam, so it shows an association, not proof. My log shows ~15 h 25 min logged toward that midsem (≈9 h lectures and study, 6 h 30 revision), steady rather than crammed.
  • When I study: mostly 22:00–01:00, with a second peak around 18:00–20:00.

The real lesson

I often didn't follow the exact order of sessions, and that was fine. The value was the forecast: which subject needs attention, how much must be done by when, and when I'd finish at my current pace. That creates urgency early, before a deadline becomes a crisis. The plan is a forecast, not a timetable.

Messy real data

I fell ill before DAA and covered several tasks during revision, then marked them done with almost no time logged. Any "learning" from the log has to recognise such items as covered elsewhere (under 25% of the estimate logged) instead of concluding I'm 5× faster.

12. Limitations (know these cold)

  • Estimates are the user's. HeadStart doesn't learn task durations (a pace feature was tried and removed); bad estimates mean a wrong forecast.
  • Tested by one user (me). No user study yet.
  • Sessions never cross midnight; a slot to 24:00 and one from 00:00 are separate days.
  • Two-stage decomposition can miss the true global optimum in rare cases (fixed by feedback rounds, not proven optimal overall). Stage 1 stops at a 1% gap.
  • Weights, not true lexicographic priorities: chosen to be far apart, but in theory a huge amount of a lower goal could outweigh a higher one.
  • No login or sync: real use is one computer; the phone needs the laptop on and the same network. Plain http locally.
  • Free hosting sleeps (≈1 min wake) and is slow for large planners.
  • Setup needs Python (about 60–80 MB of packages, mainly OR-Tools).

13. Viva questions

The project

What problem does HeadStart solve, and for whom?

Students who want to study regularly but don't know how much time to give each subject, waste time making schedules, or give up when a plan breaks. HeadStart turns classes, exams, assignments and free time into a plan that meets every deadline within daily limits, and re-plans whenever something changes.

How is it different from Google Calendar or a to-do app?

Those store what you schedule. HeadStart decides the schedule: it knows how much work is left, the deadlines and your free time, and solves for a plan. It also generates lecture study from your timetable, warns when work can't fit, and re-plans automatically.

Did it actually help you?

Yes: my CMO midsem went from 16.5/40 to 32/45 (median 23). It's one student and a different exam, so it's an association, not proof. My log shows ~15 h of steady study toward it. The biggest help was the workload forecast creating urgency early.

Is it too personal to help others?

The problem is universal; my preferences are settings: free-time grid, daily limits, lectures on/off per subject, session lengths, task types. Anyone can set up their own semester from the first screen. It hasn't been tested by other students yet, which is the honest next step.

What would you do next?
  • Let 3–5 classmates use it for a week and collect feedback.
  • Strengthen the forecast: "you're 3 h behind on DAA this week".
  • Accounts and sync if it became a product (a cloud database and a paid server).

Maths and optimisation

How did you model planning mathematically?

As an integer optimisation problem on a 10-minute grid. Variables are how many slots of each item go on each day (stage 1) and which slot each piece takes (stage 2). Constraints are linear: deadlines, capacity with breaks, daily limits, session lengths, dependencies. The objective is a weighted sum: unscheduled work is worst, then missed hobby targets, broken order, using the buffer, under-minimum days, unevenness, lateness and moving things from the previous plan.

What is CP-SAT and how does it work?

Google OR-Tools' constraint programming solver. It encodes integer variables and constraints so a SAT solver with clause learning can search, adds propagation and LP relaxations for bounds, and runs several strategies in parallel. It returns the best plan found and a proven bound, so it can say "optimal" or "within 1%".

Is the problem NP-hard? Then how is it fast?

In general yes: it contains bin packing (fitting session-sized pieces into windows). But NP-hard means no fast algorithm for every instance; real instances of this size (a few thousand variables) solve in about a second. I also keep models small: day totals in stage 1, per-day models in stage 2.

Why two stages instead of one model?

A single slot-level model over five weeks is very large and too slow to re-solve on every click. Splitting "how much per day" from "exactly when in the day" keeps each part small, lets the 35 days solve in parallel, and a feedback loop (cap a day at what fit, re-solve) handles the rare mismatch. The trade-off: no proof of a global optimum.

How do you handle "impossible" plans, e.g. too much work?

Every goal that can fail has a slack variable with a large cost, so the model is always feasible. Non-zero slacks become the problem report, e.g. "2 h of work doesn't fit before Oct 12; raise a day's limit or cut work". Nothing is ever squeezed in beyond your limits.

How do you represent |x − c| in a linear model?

Add a variable dev with dev ≥ x − c and dev ≥ c − x, and minimise it. I use this for even spread (steady items) and for stability (distance from the previous plan).

How do the objective weights work? Why those numbers?

They encode priorities: 10,000 for unscheduled work down to 1 for lateness and moving work. They are spaced so a higher goal always dominates in realistic plans, approximating a lexicographic objective in one solve. The exact values were tuned by using it (e.g. moving within a day costs 20, more than starting earlier saves).

How is "as soon as possible" expressed?

Each slot of item u on day d costs (d − earliest day) × urgency × subject weight. Urgency is 5 for a deadline within 3 days down to 1 beyond 4 weeks, so urgent work is pulled earliest.

How are dependencies modelled?

If B is used on day d, everything of A must be done by the end of d: Σ_{d'≤d} a[A,d'] + unscheduled[A] ≥ need[A], enforced only if B runs that day. A soft dependency has a boolean broken that switches it off at a cost of 500 per slot; same-day order is handled in stage 2.

What does the 1% gap mean?

Stage 1 stops once the plan's cost is proven within 1% of the best possible. Proving the last 1% took 3–4× as long and only moved work by minutes, so it isn't worth it on every click.

Why a 10-minute grid?

Real sessions and breaks are multiples of 10 minutes; a finer grid multiplies variables (1-min would be 10×) with no practical benefit.

Engineering

What happens if the solver runs out of time?

Stage 1 returns its best plan so far (always feasible thanks to slacks). Stage 2 keeps a rule-respecting greedy layout or the solver's answer, whichever placed more, never an empty day. That rule came from a real bug where a packed day came out empty.

How do you keep re-plans stable?

The previous plan is a hint for the solver and a small cost is paid for every slot moved (1 between days, 20 within a day). Re-planning with nothing changed moves nothing.

How did you test it?

Daily real use; scripted checks that every planned day obeys the stage-2 rules (session lengths, breaks, order, allowed slots); end-to-end tests through the web API on copies of my data; and a headless browser driving the real pages (including a phone-sized screen) to click through features and take screenshots.

Why JSON files and not a database?

One user, small data, human-readable, easy to back up and diff in git. The plan is recomputed from the setup and the progress log, so there's no state to corrupt. A multi-user product would need a database.

How fast is it?

For my real semester: stage 1 has ~2,200 variables and ~3,800 constraints, solved optimally in ~1 s; 35 day models in parallel; a full re-plan in about 5 s on my laptop. Small planners re-plan in about a second.

Data and insights

What does HeadStart learn from your data?

It reports, rather than learns: hours per day, streaks, effort per exam by type of work, and when in the day you study. I built a "pace" feature that learned real task durations, found it wasn't useful in practice (my estimates were already close), and removed it.

How does "When you study" work?

A log entry is saved when a session ends, so the session is taken to start "time spent" earlier, and its minutes are spread over the hours it covered. It counts hours, not sessions, and finds the 3-hour window (wrapping past midnight) with the most study. It's approximate, and the page says so.

How did you handle messy data?

Tasks I marked done with under 25% of their estimate logged (covered during revision while ill) were treated as "covered elsewhere" rather than as evidence I'm faster.

Deployment, privacy, security

Is it really deployed if the site is only a demo?

Yes: the full app runs in production on Render. Limiting the public site to a demo was a deliberate privacy decision; per-user online planners were built and removed. Anyone can run their own copy from GitHub.

What data do you store?

Online: nothing persistent from visitors (the demo is wiped on restart). Locally: your setup and progress log only. No accounts, names, emails, cookies or analytics.

How does the phone version work, and is it secure?

The laptop serves HeadStart on the local network (--lan); the phone opens it via a QR code and adds it to the home screen. It's the same planner, no copy. It's plain http without login, so it's meant for a private network like a phone hotspot; anyone on the same network with the address could open it.

Why Render and not Hugging Face or another host?

Hugging Face Docker Spaces turned out to need a paid plan; Render's free plan runs Docker with no card. The catch is sleeping after 15 minutes idle and a slow CPU. For a real product I'd use a paid instance.

How would you scale it to many students?

Accounts and a database instead of files; a job queue so solves don't block the web server; a paid instance with more CPU (each solve is seconds of CPU); caching plans and re-solving only on change, which it already does.

Using AI (Claude Code)

How much of this did AI write, and what did you do?

Claude Code wrote most of the code. I owned the product: the student-centred redesign, exams as milestones, auto lectures, as-soon-as-possible planning, the demo-only privacy decision, phone use, and removing features that didn't help. I used it every day and found the real bugs by using it. The full conversation is in my transcript.

Give an example where you changed the AI's direction.

The online version went from "anyone can start a planner" to "demo only", because I didn't want to store others' data. Another: the solver's warnings ("10 min doesn't fit") looked false; investigating showed a real 2-hour shortfall before my midsems, and I raised one day's limit.
